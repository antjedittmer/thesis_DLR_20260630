\chapter{Conclusions and Outlook} \label{Conclusions}
This thesis has shown that predictive control based on quasi-linear parameter-varying models is fast enough for real-time wind turbine control and, without model mismatch, considerably reduces structural loads and improves power reference tracking compared to established baseline schemes. On the higher-fidelity FAST model, the wind turbine qLMPC retains a large part of the power smoothing, but its load reduction depends on an accurate prediction model. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that Koopman-based MPC algorithms achieve smooth power reference tracking in wind farm control. Section~\ref{sec:Conclusions} summarizes these results, and Section~\ref{sec:Outlook} discusses resulting directions for future research.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:Conclusions}
This dissertation develops qLPV and structured low-order control schemes, as well as data-driven control approaches, for the optimization of wind energy conversion systems at the individual turbine and wind farm levels. The challenge addressed is the mitigation of potentially damaging mechanical loads and the maximization of power yield in the context of increasingly larger and slimmer turbine structures and dense wind farm layouts. This work proposes velocity-based qLMPC for turbine control to manage the challenges due to the nonlinearity of wind turbine models. For farm control, data-driven MPC algorithms based on Koopman operator theory are used, as the high dimensionality of the ODEs obtained from discretizing the flow equations of the wake interaction is an obstacle to real-time physics-based MPC. The aim for both turbine and farm control is to minimize the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCoE) by balancing control efficiency, actuator usage, and structural integrity. All control algorithms in this work are real-time capable, with mean computation times well below the sample time of turbine controllers, which is typically around 10\,ms.

A contribution of this thesis is the derivation and validation of qLPV wind turbine models that achieve a high level of fidelity relative to the widely used FAST simulation environment. Two specific model architectures were investigated: a model capturing rotor speed and tower movement, and an augmented version incorporating blade out-of-plane, generator and slip angle states. While the latter demonstrated superior open-loop fit in single-axis comparisons, closed-loop evaluations revealed only marginal performance gains. Nevertheless, the more complex model was selected as the plant for the qLMPC design, as its implementation did not lead to a measurable computational penalty, while the prediction model consists of a subset of its states close to an established model from the literature \cite{Bianchi2007}; for the application to FAST, the prediction model was extended by the tower and blade flap dynamics of this model.

In the individual turbine control domain, a velocity-based MIMO qLMPC design was benchmarked against a collective pitch and torque SISO baseline. The investigation further extended to modern load alleviation techniques, incorporating Individual Pitch Control (IPC) and aerodynamically modeled Trailing Edge Flap Control (TEFC). All configurations maintain the mean power output of the baseline. Without model mismatch, the qLMPC reduces the standard deviations of the tower fore-aft acceleration and of the power tracking error by factors of up to 4.1 and 11.4. On FAST, the adapted qLMPC designs still reduce the power fluctuations, by about a third in the comparison of all controllers, which no other approach achieves, but they increase the tower DEL and do not reduce the blade DEL. IPC and the combined IPC-TEFC, referred to as Active Aerodynamic Load Control (AALC), reduce the blade DEL above rated by 21\% to 24\% for the optimization dataset and two verification datasets, with gains optimized at a single wind speed. AALC achieves this at a considerably lower actuator power than IPC by offloading part of the load reduction onto the flaps. Combining the qLMPC with IPC yields the only controller in this comparison that reduces both the blade DEL and the power fluctuations. These results underline the potential of qLMPC, but also its dependence on an accurate prediction model.

At the wind farm level, this thesis introduces an adaptive control framework within the WFSim environment to manage wake interactions. The transition to farm-level control necessitated a data-driven algorithm based on Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and Koopman-inspired lifting functions. A data-driven, adaptive Axial Induction Control (AIC) allows the controller to estimate effective wind speeds and track power references that were unattainable for a non-adaptive scheme trained on the same dataset that was deliberately chosen not to cover the whole operating range. By selectively updating the model only when significant discrepancies are detected, the framework maintains a Variance-Accounted-For (VAF) of approximately 90\% in both open- and closed-loop scenarios.

Furthermore, the integration of WRC and AIC was explored through two methodologies. The first approach leverages the analytical Bastankhah-Gaussian wake model to pre-optimize yaw misalignment, while the second utilizes a combined WRC-AIC Koopman MPC to simultaneously optimize thrust and yaw in a unified multivariable formulation. This simultaneous optimization proved particularly effective in simulations, demonstrating that data-driven models can accurately capture the effects of induction and redirection to optimize total farm yield.

\section{Outlook} \label{sec:Outlook}
Despite the promising results, several idealized assumptions within the simulation framework 
must be addressed to transition to real-world application. The individual turbine 
simulations were conducted under the assumption that the wind at the turbine is perfectly 
known. Moreover, the application of the qLMPC to FAST showed that structural modes not contained in the prediction model deteriorate its load reduction. A qLPV prediction model stitched from linearized FAST models could reduce this mismatch and recover at least part of the tower load reduction obtained without model mismatch. While wind estimators are routinely used in industry, their accuracy is inherently 
sensitive to turbine parameters that inevitably change as the system ages. The integration 
of sensors such as spinner anemometers and lidar offers an alternative, and 
future work should explore lidar-based wind preview to enhance both CPC and IPC MPC schemes, 
particularly for gust alleviation. Both pitch and generator 
torque dynamics were modeled using first-order transfer functions with constant parameters. 
In practice, these mechanical responses are subject to degradation over time, suggesting 
that learning and adapting to changed actuator behavior is a necessary research extension.

The study focused on wind steps and normal turbulent conditions. The results 
presented in~\cite{dittmer2026koopman} extend the WFSim environment to support turbulent 
inflow using the Kaimal Normal Turbulence Model, demonstrating that while turbulence reduces 
the potential gains from WRC compared to steady-state predictions, the 
combined WRC-AIC scheme still outperforms baseline AIC, reducing power tracking error 
considerably under realistic, time-varying wind conditions. However, load evaluation under WRC-induced yaw misalignment remain open problems, which cannot be addressed with WFSim's actuator disk turbine model. This quasi-static turbine model, which represents the WESC as an energy sink without any mechanical properties nor delays, is also not capable to imitate the dynamic behavior required to fully evaluate power demand following. Transitioning to environments such 
as FAST.Farm would allow for the inclusion of dynamic turbine behavior, a comprehensive 
evaluation of loads during WRC, and the investigation of Dynamic Induction Control~(DIC) 
alongside AIC and WRC. To prove industrial validity, the framework should ultimately be 
tested on a higher number of turbines under realistic, varying wind directions, in 
higher-fidelity simulations and be validated with measurement data. Research wind farms such as WiValdi, 
operated by DLR  together with its partners in the Research 
Alliance Wind Energy, provide the instrumentation required for 
the latter: inflow measurements from meteorological masts and lidar, together with load 
measurements on instrumented turbines, would allow both the effective wind speed estimates 
and the load reductions predicted here to be verified experimentally.

Another research opportunity lies in defining a concrete financial advantage for load 
alleviation against the disadvantage of increased actuator wear. Quantifying this trade-off 
is essential for determining the optimal LCoE in a commercially viable controller.

On the modeling side, the physically motivated lifting functions selected in this thesis 
were shown to yield accurate Koopman models for wind farm control. Subsequent 
work~\cite{sharan2024autoencoder, sharan2026tcn}, to which the author contributed, has 
explored neural network-based lifting functions as an alternative, suggesting that learned 
observables can outperform manually selected polynomial functions, particularly under varying 
operating conditions. This direction is promising for larger farms and more complex inflow 
scenarios, where the physical relationships underpinning the polynomial observables used 
here become harder to specify a priori.

The proposed methodologies reveal great potential for data-driven multivariable control to solve the increasingly complex trade-offs in modern wind energy production. By interpreting these control laws as a combination of structured low-order loops and high-level predictive optimization, the path toward an industrial-scale implementation of model predictive wind turbine and farm control becomes clearer and more computationally accessible.
